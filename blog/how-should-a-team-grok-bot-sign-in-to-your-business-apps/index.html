How Should a Team Grok Bot Sign In to Your Business Apps?

November 16, 2026 · By David McCormick · 7 min read

Match the login to the job. A Team Grok Bot can use each teammate's own account, one shared account set up by a Manager, or a separate account of its own. Use individual logins for anything personal like email and calendars, a dedicated Bot account for shared business tools where the app supports it, and share your own login only for accounts that hold nothing sensitive or private.

What changed in how Team Bots sign in to apps?

Managers can now share one app login with the whole Bot, if a team admin allows it. The Grok Bot changelog entry for November 11 says owners and Managers can share their own app login as a shared connector once a team admin turns on Allow Shared Logins. Before this, each teammate generally had to connect an app themselves before the Bot could use it in their chats.

The Team Bots documentation lays out the rule that matters most: a connected account acts as the person the Bot is answering, and one teammate's connection is never used for someone else unless a Manager shares it as a shared connector. That keeps personal accounts personal by default, and makes sharing a deliberate choice.

What are the three ways a Team Bot can sign in?

Individual accounts, a shared account, or the Bot's own account. The owner or a Manager opens the Bot's details, goes to Setup, chooses Service accounts, and picks an app under Shared connectors. From there:

  • Use individual accounts. Each teammate connects their own. The Bot works as whoever it is answering. Best for email, calendars, and anything tied to one person.
  • Share your account. Everyone who uses the Bot acts through the Manager's account, including to make changes. You connect the app for yourself first. The docs advise sharing only an account that holds nothing sensitive or private.
  • The Bot's own account. A separate account the Bot uses for the team. This is usually the cleanest choice for shared business tools, though some apps do not support it yet.

The app's row shows whose account the Bot is using, so anyone on the team can check.

When does a shared login make sense for a small business?

When the account is already a team account and the Bot's job is narrow. A shared login works well for tools your whole team already uses through one business account, like a shared project board or a reporting dashboard, where every action is something you would be comfortable with any teammate doing.

It is a poor fit for a personal inbox, a personal calendar, banking, payroll, or any account where actions carry one person's name. Remember that everything the Bot does through a shared login shows up as that Manager. If a client sees an email or a CRM note, it looks like it came from that person. When in doubt, give the Bot its own account or keep logins individual.

What should you check before you share a login with a Bot?

Check what the account can reach, who can talk to the Bot, and what needs approval. A short checklist before you turn on a shared connector:

  1. Review the account's permissions. If the login can delete records, issue refunds, or export customer lists, the Bot can too. Use a role with only what the job needs.
  2. Check who has access to the Bot. Admins can see and change who can use each Team Bot. Everyone with access effectively gets that account's reach through the Bot.
  3. Keep approvals on for changes. Let the Bot read freely, but require a yes before it sends messages to customers, edits records in bulk, or spends money.
  4. Review what the Bot learns. The same release added Review learnings, where what teammates teach a Team Bot waits until the owner or a Manager approves it. Use it so one person's shortcut does not quietly become the team's rule.
  5. Plan for reconnects. If a shared app needs reconnecting, only the Manager whose account it is can do it. Make sure that person knows they own it.

How does this apply to your CRM, inbox, and ad accounts?

Treat each system differently based on whose name is on the work. For a CRM like GoHighLevel, a dedicated user for the Bot with limited permissions keeps its notes, tasks, and pipeline moves clearly labeled and easy to audit. For inboxes, keep connections individual so replies come from the right person, and route customer-facing drafts through approval. For ad platforms, read-only reporting access covers most of what a Bot needs to summarize spend and results, and budget changes should stay with a person.

Frequently asked questions

What is a shared connector in Grok Bot?

It is an app connection on a Team Bot that everyone uses through one account, so teammates do not each have to connect the app. The owner or a Manager sets it up under the Bot's Setup, in Service accounts.

Who can turn on Allow Shared Logins?

A team admin. It is off by default and sits under Sharing on the Grok Bot page of the dashboard. Until it is on, Managers who try to share a login see a message that the admin has turned off sharing logins with Bots.

Does a Team Bot use my personal Gmail or calendar without asking?

No. In your own chat with a Team Bot, it asks before it uses one of your personal accounts, and you can allow once, always allow, or skip. In Slack channels and group chats it uses only its owner's apps and asks before using yours.

What happens if a shared login expires?

Only the Manager whose account is shared can reconnect it, under the Bot's Shared connectors. Plan for that person to be reachable, or use the Bot's own account where the app supports it.

Can I stop sharing a login later?

Yes. Choose Stop sharing on the app's row or remove the app. Teammates then connect their own accounts.
